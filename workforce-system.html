California’s local workforce system

How California’s local workforce system works

California has 45 local workforce development boards. Each is led by local business and community leaders who decide how to invest and coordinate federal, state, and local resources to meet the needs of employers and workers in their region.

The short version

A national system, run through local decisions.

The Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act (WIOA), signed in 2014, is the federal law behind the public workforce system. Its largest piece, Title I, funds services for adults, dislocated workers, and young people. Congress sets the funding, the U.S. Department of Labor distributes it to states by formula, and states pass most of it to local workforce development areas.

Each local area has a local workforce development board. The board is appointed by the area’s chief local elected officials, a majority of its members come from business, and it is chaired by a business representative. The board decides how the area’s workforce dollars are invested, which training and services to fund, and how to coordinate with community colleges, adult schools, economic development, social services, and employers.

California’s 45 local areas range from a single city to eleven counties. Together they cover all 58 counties. Some boards are staffed by a county or city, others by a joint powers authority or a nonprofit, but every one answers to local elected officials and reports on the same federal measures.

Who leads a local board

Appointed locally and led by business.

Federal law sets the makeup of every local board so that the people who hire workers and the people who train them sit at the same table. In all, 976 members serve on California’s 45 boards.

How the money moves

From Congress to a local decision.

WIOA Title I dollars follow a set path. The final decision on how they are spent sits with the local board.

Congress

Appropriates

Sets annual funding for the Title I adult, dislocated worker, and youth programs.

U.S. Department of Labor

Allots to states

Distributes funds to states by formulas based largely on unemployment and poverty.

State of California

Allocates to local areas

The state may reserve up to 15 percent for statewide activities, plus up to 25 percent of dislocated worker funds for layoff response, and allocates the rest to local areas by formula.

Local board

Invests

Decides how the area’s funds are spent on training, career services, youth programs, and employer services.

Who boards serve

People looking for work and employers ready to hire.

Anyone can walk into an America’s Job Center of California for help. Title I training and intensive services are focused on the groups federal law names.

Adults

Ages 18 and older

Priority goes to people receiving public assistance, other people with low incomes, and people whose basic skills are holding them back. Veterans and eligible spouses receive priority across programs.

Dislocated workers

People who lost a job

Workers who were laid off or received a layoff notice, people whose business closed because of economic conditions, and displaced homemakers who need to return to work.

Youth

Ages 14 to 24

Young people facing barriers to school or work. Federal law directs most youth funding to young people who are out of school, and at least 20 percent to paid and unpaid work experience.

Employers

Businesses of every size

Recruiting and screening, training current and new workers, help through a layoff, and labor market information for planning.

What local boards do

Set strategy, choose partners, and invest.

Boards are responsible for the local system as a whole. Most services reach people through partners the board selects and oversees.

1

Plan for the regional economy

Every board writes a four-year local plan built on labor market analysis, and works with neighboring boards on a regional plan. California has 15 regional planning units.

2

Run the job center network

Boards certify America’s Job Center of California locations, competitively select the operator that coordinates them, and bring required partners such as adult education, the state employment service, and vocational rehabilitation under one roof.

3

Invest in training that leads to jobs

Boards fund training through accounts people use with approved providers, on-the-job training with local employers, apprenticeships, and training for current workers so they can move up and keep their jobs.

4

Work alongside employers

Boards convene employers by industry, learn what skills they need, and build training and hiring around those jobs.

5

Respond when layoffs hit

When a layoff notice arrives, board staff can be on site within days to help workers file for benefits, find training, and connect to their next job.

6

Oversee and report

Boards set the local budget in public meetings, monitor the providers they fund, and report results to the state and the U.S. Department of Labor.

Accountable for results

No two boards make the same choices. Every one reports on the same measures.

Each board negotiates performance targets with the state on six federal indicators, adjusted for local economic conditions and the people it serves.

One state, many economies

Why the decisions are made locally.

A forestry crew, a semiconductor technician, and a home health aide all need different training, and the regions that employ them face different labor markets. California alone shows how wide those differences run.

Ours is a large and diverse nation where the investments communities need vary widely. The strength of the federal workforce system is a structure in which local business and community leaders decide how to invest and coordinate federal, state, and local resources.

Partners in the system

Who does what in California.

LWDA

Labor and Workforce Development Agency

The cabinet-level agency that oversees the state’s labor and workforce departments.

CWDB

California Workforce Development Board

The state board. It writes California’s Unified Strategic Workforce Development Plan and sets statewide policy.

EDD

Employment Development Department

Administers WIOA funds in California, runs the state employment service, and publishes labor market data.

CWA

California Workforce Association

The organization representing local workforce development boards in California. CWA helps boards learn from one another and represents their shared priorities in Sacramento and Washington, D.C.

What’s ahead

Decisions coming before Congress and Sacramento.

Federal funding

Adjusted for inflation, national WIOA Title I funding is 17.9 percent lower than in program year 2015. CWA asks Congress to fund Title I at no less than fiscal year 2026 levels.

Reauthorizing WIOA

WIOA’s authorization has expired, and Congress continues to fund it each year. CWA asks that reauthorization trust local business and community leaders to decide how to invest.

New work requirements

From January 1, 2027, about 3.5 million Medi-Cal enrollees become newly subject to an 80-hour monthly requirement of work, training, education, or service. Local boards will be one of the places they turn, and CWA asks that boards be funded for that work.

Questions

Common questions from new offices.

Is a local board part of county government?

It depends on the area. The board itself is appointed by the area’s chief local elected officials. Its staff may work for a county, a city, a joint powers authority formed by several local governments, or a nonprofit.

What is the difference between a local board and an America’s Job Center of California?

The board sets strategy, decides how funds are invested, and oversees the system. America’s Job Center of California locations are where people and employers receive services, through an operator and providers the board selects.

How do local boards relate to the state workforce board?

The California Workforce Development Board sets statewide policy and the state plan. Local boards carry out that plan in their regions and decide how local funds are invested to meet the needs of their own economies.

How can my office connect with the boards in my district?

CWA can introduce you to the directors of the boards that serve your district, arrange a briefing for you or your staff, and set up a visit to a local job center.
